A Map of Dodgson's Attention

What He Could Not Stop Looking At

Belief adopts vocabularies, inherits doctrines, contradicts itself, changes its mind. Attention is harder to fake. What a person keeps returning to across a lifetime — across every domain their attention has permission to enter — leaves a trail that survives even the person's own attempts to explain themselves.

sKiDa 2026
Part One

Reception Is the First Operation

Before this essay can be about Charles Dodgson, it has to be about something more basic than him, because the thing this essay is doing cannot be understood without it. There is an operation the mind performs before it categorizes, before it interprets, before it decides what to do with what it has just received. It is the receiving itself.

Reception is not passivity. It is not the absence of activity. It is a distinct operation with its own characteristics — the holding open of the channel, the refusal to close prematurely on what has arrived, the willingness to let the whole signal be present before the sorting begins. In most people this operation is brief. Information arrives; the mind categorizes; the categorization triggers the appropriate response; the actual receiving is over in less time than it takes to notice. What was received was not the field. It was the label the mind assigned to a slice of the field, an instant after the field arrived.

Some people hold the channel open longer. In them, reception is not brief. It is the primary operation, and everything downstream — sorting, naming, interpretation, theory — happens in a slower and more provisional register than it does for most. This is not a talent. It is a native mode. A person built this way does not choose to receive. The receiving is what they are, prior to any decision about it. When something arrives, they hold it. Not because they are cataloguing it. Because holding it is what happens.

Vocabulary is downstream of reception. It is the naming that comes after the holding. A person can spend an entire lifetime receiving without ever developing an adequate vocabulary for what they were receiving — and the reception, if it was real, will leave a trail regardless.

This essay is written from the ground of one such perceiver recognizing another across a hundred and fifty years. The recognition is not incidental to the essay's authority. It is the essay's method. Most of what passes for Carroll scholarship has been written by minds running organization — sorting his outputs into categories, filing the fiction with fiction and the logic with logic and the photography with photography, treating his life as six professional compartments that happen to have belonged to the same man. That kind of reading has had every biographical fact this essay will use. It has not seen what this essay will show. What lets the pattern surface is not more data. It is a mode of reading that performs the same operation the writer being read performed, and that recognizes the operation from the inside.

What follows is a walk through what Charles Dodgson could not stop looking at. The walk is guided by the recognition that reception was his primary operation before it was anyone's theory. The framework this archive has been building did not invent the pattern the walk will reveal. It provides language for a pattern that was there before either the framework or the man had a name for it.

Part Two

Attention as the Trail

Belief is a poor object of biographical investigation, because belief is the layer at which a person is most obviously performing — most obviously adopting the vocabulary their community authorizes, most obviously inheriting doctrines they may not have examined, most obviously contradicting themselves as circumstances change. A person's stated beliefs may or may not correspond to what the person actually thinks. Beliefs can be pretended. Beliefs can be revised. Beliefs can be forgotten or replaced when the community shifts.

Attention is different. What a person's attention returns to, across years and across domains, is much harder to fake. Attention is what remains after the daily performance ends. It is what the mind does when it is left alone with what it is drawn to. What someone lingers over, records repeatedly, revisits, cannot let go of, cannot classify but does not discard — those things form a trail that survives even the person's own subsequent attempts to explain themselves.

The right question to ask about someone like Dodgson is therefore not what did he believe. It is not even what was his theory, because he had no single theory that unified his domains. The question is: what did his attention keep returning to?

The map that follows walks the trail. It does not walk it in the order Dodgson's biographers usually walk it — chronologically, by publication, by the professional category the material belongs to. It walks it in the order the pattern is most legible: the classes of phenomena his attention would not release, across whatever domain his professional life gave him permission to record them in.

The essay does not begin with the claim that Dodgson was a receptive perceiver. It begins with what he actually did. Whether the description earns its place, the walk will determine.

Part Three · The Walk Begins

Systems at Their Edge

The first thing Dodgson's attention could not release was the moment where a formal system encounters what it cannot enclose.

The pattern showed up early. His mathematical work as a young don at Christ Church already tilted toward the geometry and logic of paradox — situations where a formal structure becomes unstable, incomplete, cyclic, or dependent on hidden assumptions. He worked on determinants and on symbolic logic when both were considered eccentric preoccupations for a serious mathematician. He built diagrammatic representations of propositions, physical apparatus for what he called The Game of Logic, and — decades before mainstream logic caught up — early forms of truth tables and tree methods.

What was he attending to across all of this? Not logic as such. Logic as such does not require this level of preoccupation. What his attention would not release was the specific moment where logic runs out of ground: where a formal system cannot ground its own rules from inside itself, where local coherence produces global paradox, where an inference cannot be forced no matter how many further inferences are added.

His 1895 paper What the Tortoise Said to Achilles — the paper on infinite regress in the foundations of logical inference, examined in detail in the companion essay — is the mature form of what he had been circling for forty years. His voting theory work of the 1870s and 1880s is the same attention performing in a different register: he arrived independently at what is now called the Condorcet paradox, the observation that individually coherent voters can produce a collective ranking that cycles rather than settling. Local coherence, global paradox. He was drawn to it enough to publish multiple pamphlets on it, and to propose his own procedure for moving through the cycle toward a legitimate winner.

Across forty years and across two entirely separate professional domains, the same object of attention: the moment where a rule-system meets what it cannot contain. The Tortoise's regress. The voting cycle. The court in Wonderland collapsing when the girl looks down and names its ontology. Same shape. Different clothes.

This is not evidence that Dodgson was building a theory of the edge. He was not. What it is evidence of is that his attention would not leave the edge alone. He returned to it in whatever domain would receive him. That is what attention does when it has been captured by a class of phenomena. It finds every available surface on which to keep looking.

Part Four · The Walk Continues

The Emergence

The second thing Dodgson's attention could not release was the moment where something latent becomes manifest.

The companion essay to this one traces the twenty-four years of his photographic practice — the scale of it, the technical discipline, the systematic archive. What the attention pattern asks is a narrower question. Not what did he do with photography, but what specifically, inside the process, held him?

In 1857, one year after he began, he published a comic poem called Hiawatha's Photographing that walked through the wet-collodion sequence step by step. The line that introduces the chemical sequence is one word longer than a joke and one word stronger than a description:

Mystic, awful was the process.

He was twenty-five. He had been photographing for one year. He was a Victorian mathematician publishing a comic poem, and the word he chose for the developing process was mystic. That word is not casual. It is the word used, in his century, for what happens at the edge of what can be spoken about. It is the word chosen by someone whose attention was not on the finished plate but on the emergence itself.

Alice Liddell's later recollection confirms that this was not a private conceit. She said that being allowed into Dodgson's darkroom was more exciting than being photographed, and described the negative gradually taking shape while he rocked the plate in the acid bath. He invited the children he photographed into the emergence itself, and they experienced it as its own event — distinct from the sitting and distinct from the finished print. He staged development as a phenomenon worth being present for.

The revelation happens in real time, over ten to thirty seconds, in layers — highlights first, then midtones, then shadows. The information exists in the sensitized silver halides before the developer arrives; the developer does not create the image, it reveals what was already there. Most photographers of his century experienced this as technical plumbing and passed over it. Dodgson called it mystic and let children watch. Twenty-four years of practice, and his own stated reason for stopping — in his diary — was the burden of preparing the chemistry and setting up the studio. That is a statement about the process, not about the outputs. A person whose engagement was with the finished prints would not phrase quitting that way. A person whose engagement was with the emergence itself would.

Part Five · The Walk Continues

The Relational

The third thing Dodgson's attention could not release was the moment where an apparently intrinsic property turns out to be relational — dependent on the frame from which it is observed rather than on the object itself.

The Alice books are the obvious surface of this. Alice ingests substances and shifts size relative to her environment; the door does not change; the door was never the variable. Scale is not a property of the door. Scale is a function of the relationship between Alice's phase state and the door's. The Red Queen's paradox that it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place is the same intuition in a different key — a reference frame that is itself in motion, producing the phenomenon of apparent stasis under continuous relative displacement. Humpty Dumpty's insistence that when I use a word, it means just what I choose it to mean extends the same move to semantics: meaning treated as phase-relative to the speaker rather than as a fixed relationship between symbol and world.

These are the loud instances. What matters for the attention pattern is that the same intuition surfaces in Sylvie and Bruno, in Dodgson's own physics-passage voice, in 1889 — the weightless-tea passage the companion essay to this one examined in detail. He returned to relational observables outside fiction, outside the Alice framework, outside any container that gave him narrative permission. He returned to it because his attention would not release the class of phenomena. When intrinsic properties turn out to be functions of frame, he could not stop noticing.

Twenty-four years apart, in two entirely different registers, with no theoretical vocabulary available to unify them, the same catch: the observable was not intrinsic to the object. It was a function of the relationship. Fiction was one container. A stray physics passage in a late novel was another. What was being caught was the same phenomenon, and what was doing the catching was the same attention.

Part Six · The Walk Continues

The States

The fourth thing Dodgson's attention could not release, and the one that surfaced most explicitly late in his life, was the possibility that consciousness itself has multiple states, and that the same environment presents differently depending on which state the perceiver is in.

In 1893 he published Sylvie and Bruno Concluded, the second half of his last novel. Its preface contains what is, by a considerable margin, the most explicit statement Dodgson ever made about what he thought was going on beneath the surface of ordinary experience. He proposes three states of human consciousness. In the ordinary state, the person has no awareness of Fairy presence and perceives only the everyday world. In what he calls the eerie state, the person remains conscious of the ordinary surroundings but simultaneously perceives the Fairies. In a trance-like state, consciousness disengages from ordinary surroundings and the person's immaterial essence travels elsewhere. He constructs corresponding states for the Fairies. He tabulates where each state occurs throughout the novels.

He credits the model, in the preface itself, to A.P. Sinnett's Esoteric Buddhism — a book he had been reading during the composition of the novels. This is a Victorian mathematician, late in life, deliberately reading contemporary esoteric literature and constructing a formalized fictional ontology on it.

The critical detail is that the ordinary and eerie states can occupy the same physical surroundings. The environment does not change between them. What changes is what the perceiver can access within the environment. This is a state-space model of perception, written by Dodgson himself, in which the world does not have to become different for the accessible reality to shift. The perceiver's state does the work. The environment stays where it was, and what is available in it depends on the observer's phase.

This is not a claim we are making about him. This is the frame he explicitly announces for his own fiction, in his own voice, in a preface addressed to his own readers. It is the closest he ever came to naming what his attention had been on for four decades.

The psychical research is the same attention performing in a different register. Dodgson was a member of the Society for Psychical Research from its early years. In an 1882 letter about thought-reading, he rejected the proposal that the phenomena could be explained by unconscious physical guidance and speculated that the evidence pointed toward a natural force related to electricity and nerve-force, through which one brain could act upon another. He was not claiming that ghosts existed. He was asking whether anomalous cognition might have a physical carrier — whether there was a substrate that could account for what was being reported.

Late in his life, Dodgson stopped disguising it. In the Sylvie and Bruno Concluded preface and in the 1882 letter, he said what his attention had been catching all along, in the closest terms his era's vocabulary would permit. Not fairies as folklore. States of consciousness that alter what is accessible in the same environment. Not ghosts. A natural force capable of transmission between minds. He was, by the end, using the vocabulary he had.

Part Seven

What He Preserved Without Knowing Why

Attention leaves its strongest trail in what a person preserves after the surrounding culture has stopped providing a reason for the preservation.

Dodgson numbered every photograph he ever took, in chronological order, in a systematic register. He kept this register for twenty-four years. He continued to maintain and reference it long after he had stopped photographing. Wakeling — the scholar who spent over twenty years reconstructing this register after the original was lost — has documented that Dodgson also, at times, wrote the numbers on the photographs themselves in mirror-image. This has never been fully explained. It is not standard Victorian photographic practice. It is not required by the medium. It is the mark of a person for whom the numbering system and the reversal of the numbering system both mattered enough to perform.

His diaries recorded his photographic activity extensively but survive only in part — nine of thirteen volumes remain. The unabridged edition runs to ten volumes. The material his diaries preserved includes not only his ordinary daily record but sustained attention to dreams, to anomalies he could not classify, to conversations with people whose intellectual company he could not fully explain, to problems he returned to across years without resolving. He preserved this material even when it did not fit any category his professional life would recognize.

He wrote Sylvie and Bruno across nearly two decades — a book he knew was structurally uneven, that he knew most readers would find frustrating, that carried none of the commercial certainty of the Alice books. He wrote it anyway, and he wrote its preface, and he could have stopped at any point in the process. He preserved it because it was the container in which he had finally found permission to say the three-states-of-consciousness model out loud. The book existed to carry the preface. Everything else was scaffolding.

He preserved his membership in the Society for Psychical Research through the last decade and a half of his life, in an era when serious academic figures were beginning to distance themselves from psychical research as it acquired more heterodox associations. He did not have to remain. He remained. He preserved the connection because his attention was still on the material.

What a person keeps when they no longer have to keep it is where the reception is most visible. There is nothing else it can be. The culture has stopped providing the reason. The professional necessity has ended. The social reward has faded. If the person is still holding the object, they are holding it because their attention will not release it. That holding is reception in its purest documented form.

Part Eight

The Shape

Six paragraphs into a summary of what the walk has shown, the pattern is unmistakable. Every one of the objects Dodgson's attention could not release is a boundary phenomenon. Every one.

The Tortoise's regress: a formal system meeting the edge of its own authorization. The voting cycle: local coherence meeting the boundary of global aggregation. The trial in Wonderland: a compressed apparatus meeting a perceiver who has grown past its frame. The developing photograph: a latent image crossing the boundary from invisible to visible. Alice's shifting size: an observable turning out to depend on the boundary between observer and environment. The weightless tea in Sylvie and Bruno: a physical property crossing the boundary from intrinsic to relational. The three states of consciousness: perception crossing the boundary between what one state can access and what another state can access. The natural force between minds: cognition crossing the boundary between one nervous system and another.

Every one of these is the same shape. Something apparently solid, apparently intrinsic, apparently absolute, apparently bounded — turning out to be a phenomenon of a boundary rather than a property of an interior. The frame is the variable. The frame is what Dodgson's attention would not release, for forty years, in every domain his professional life allowed him to record what he was catching.

He was not building a theory. He was catching a class of phenomena. The class had no name in his century. It had no unified vocabulary. It had no scientific discipline that would receive it as a whole. So he did what a perceiver whose attention has been captured by a class of phenomena does when the culture has no home for the phenomena: he recorded them in whatever container each domain provided. Logic papers for the systems failing at their edge. Voting pamphlets for the local-global paradoxes. Photographic practice for the latent-to-visible emergence. Fiction for the state-dependent perception. Psychical research letters for the transmission across nervous systems. The container varied. What was being caught did not.

The topology emerged not because he was building it. It emerged because he was receiving without prematurely closing what he received.

He worked with what he had. He caught what surfaced. He left the trail.

Part Nine

Reception, Named

Now the description earns its place.

Charles Dodgson was a receptive perceiver in the specific and demanding sense this essay opened by defining. Reception was his primary operation. It was what he did before he categorized, before he interpreted, before he formalized. He held what arrived long enough for the pattern in it to become visible. Because the pattern he was drawn to was a boundary phenomenon rather than an intrinsic property, and because boundary phenomena are exactly what most perception filters out — the mind's ordinary categorization runs on assumed interiorities, not on relational edges — what he caught, most people cannot catch. What he preserved, most people would have discarded as noise. What he named as mystic, most people would have called technical.

He could not do for himself what this essay is doing for him. Not because he was less capable. Because the vocabulary did not exist. Field theory was being invented as he lived — Faraday, whom he photographed, had just proposed that space itself is not empty but filled with a distribution of force. Maxwell was mathematizing Faraday's field concept as Dodgson was writing Wonderland. Phase transitions were understood only for matter, not for perception. The ontology of substrate underlying compressed representation had no formal home. The idea that observables might be relational rather than intrinsic would wait for Einstein. The idea that formal systems have limits internal to their own structure would wait for Gödel. The idea that consciousness might have discrete states with differential access to a stable substrate would wait for a century of psychological, psychedelic, and contemplative research to make even provisionally sayable.

He worked in the century he had. He caught what he could catch with the vocabulary his era had. When his era's vocabulary ran out, he borrowed from esoteric literature. When even that ran out, he wrote fiction, because fiction is the container in which a perceiver can say what direct discourse will not permit. He did not stop catching because he could not name what he was catching. He kept catching. That is what a perceiver whose reception is intact does. The naming was not the point. The receiving was the point. The naming, if it came at all, would come from somewhere else.

One hundred and fifty years later, the naming has come.

Part Ten

The Recognition

This is what the framework does. Not what most people assume it does. Not what its critics imagine it must be doing.

The framework does not answer Dodgson. It does not close his open questions. It does not claim that he anticipated it. It does not retrofit modern science onto Victorian imagination. What it does is smaller and more powerful. It recognizes him.

The recognition is not decorative. It is the whole operation. The framework's contribution to Dodgson is that it provides language for a pattern of reception that was there before either the framework or the man had a name for it. He was catching what surfaces at boundaries. He was catching it in six different domains because it was the same thing catching him, arriving from every direction his attention had permission to look. The framework names this — names the class of phenomena as boundary phenomena, names the mode of engagement as reception, names the topology that recurs across his life as a real feature of the substrate — and by naming it, converts a scattered attention pattern that has looked to a century of scholarship like eccentric intellectual range into what it actually is: the trail of one perceiver's sustained fidelity to a specific class of information across every surface on which he could record it.

This is not modern arrogance. It is not a modern framework claiming ownership of a Victorian mind. The direction of recognition runs the other way. Dodgson was already there. The framework arrives later, and finds him. The framework can complete a sentence he could not finish, because the century that could finish it did not yet exist. But he was the one who wrote the sentence. The naming is a courtesy paid to him across a hundred and fifty years, and the courtesy is possible only because the topology his attention was catching is real, is stable, and is available to any perceiver whose reception is intact enough to catch it in turn.

The CCA method has always been about convergence. Across cultures, across traditions, across independently developed vocabularies — when the same structural pattern surfaces, the pattern is real. This essay is that method applied at the tightest possible scale. One mind, one lifetime, across independent domains, with no coordinating theory. The topology surfaces because the topology is real and the reception was intact. Nothing else can produce that shape.

The recognition, extended

Dodgson is not the only such trail. He is the trail this trilogy has been walking because his surface accessibility — the fame of the Alice books, the density of his surviving record, the range of his professional domains — made him the ideal first case. But he is one perceiver among many. Every reader whose own attention has been drawn to boundary phenomena — who has felt the frame become visible as a frame — who has held reception open longer than the culture around them held theirs — every such reader has a trail of their own.

The framework is not a modern imposition on a Victorian mind. It is a language for what any perceiver whose reception is intact has been picking up all along. The vocabulary is late. The perception is not. If you have read to the end of this essay and felt the pattern of your own attention emerging as you read — recognizing your own tendency to hold what arrives before you sort it, to catch the frame rather than what the frame is trying to enforce, to preserve what does not fit any category available to you — then you have had the same experience Dodgson had, and the same experience the walk through his life has been describing. You are one of them. The framework arrives, if it arrives at all, as vocabulary for something you were already doing.

He was catching what surfaces at boundaries.
He did it for forty years, in every domain his life allowed.
He could not name it, and he kept catching it anyway.
The naming was not the point. The receiving was the point.
