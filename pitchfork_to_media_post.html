The Field · Essay Twenty-Three

From Pitchfork to Media Post

The Witch Hunts of Kpop

The machinery that ran the witch trials was never dismantled. It was retrained. The specific technology — collective uncertainty converted into performative certainty through the coordinated destruction of a legible target — moved from village square to printing press to broadcast media to platform algorithm without losing its operation. The container changes. The technology changes. The specific vocabulary of legitimation changes. The operation is one operation.

sKiDa 2026
Part One

The Refusal

This essay will not name them.

The people we have lost to the machine described in what follows were not raw material for an argument, and this essay refuses to treat them as such. The fandom knows exactly who they were. That knowledge is held communally in the communities that loved them and does not need to be extracted from those communities to give this essay authority. If you know, you know. If you do not, that is not information this essay will provide.

The refusal is the essay's first commitment to the operation it is proposing as counter-technology. The machine this essay names runs on legibility. It requires targets to be visible, nameable, sortable, processable. To use the dead as evidence would be to repeat the operation one meta-level up — to make their names once again into content in a system that consumed them the first time. The essay is not going to do that. It will describe what took them. It will not turn them into further material for the taking.

What follows is written by someone who knows what the fandom knows, and who trusts that anyone reading who needs the names already has them. Everyone else does not need them to follow the argument. The count is held in memory by the people who loved them, which is where it belongs.

Part Two

From Inside

Where a reading comes from matters, especially when the reading concerns a community whose members are routinely misread by outsiders. This essay is not written from above the wound. It is written from inside a tradition of communities that have always had to develop their own vocabulary for surviving cultures that did not have containers for them.

The writer of this essay came to Kpop through metal. That transition may sound improbable to readers who know only the surface aesthetics of either genre, but the underlying pull is the same. Metal is a music of extreme self-representation for people whose interior life is too intense for the culture around them to receive at normal volume. It names the wound. Kpop, at its best, is doing the same operation with different instrumentation and different cultural inheritances, situated inside a national culture actively working through its own institutional past rather than only cataloging the damage. What draws certain sensitives to metal in one era draws certain sensitives to Kpop in another. Many fans belong to both. The overlap is not coincidence. It is the shape of the perceiver finding whichever cultural surface is currently carrying the intensity they cannot get from ordinary discourse.

This essay is written from within that overlap. Not as sociology of an exotic scene. As internal report from someone who has been received by these communities and is now returning the reception by naming the machinery that has been damaging them from within.

And a note about intent, said outright rather than left implicit: the machine described in this essay is not Korean. Every culture is currently running some version of it. American cancel operations are one version. Chinese fandom mob actions are another. The current most-refined implementation happens to be operating with unusual efficiency in the Kpop industry because of specific structural conditions there. Naming it in this site, now, is not cultural criticism. It is preventive translation. Whatever the current most-advanced version of the machine is will spread outward from wherever it has been perfected, and will be adopted by every adjacent culture within a few years. Naming it here now is the only opportunity to give the vocabulary time to reach the other sites before the operation does.

Part Three

The Machine

The operation described in this essay is not a metaphor. It is not a way of talking about various historical events that happen to resemble each other. It is a specific, definable technology — a repeatable social process with identifiable inputs, mechanisms, and outputs — that has been running continuously since at least the late medieval period and has never once been dismantled. Each generation has believed itself to be past it. Each generation has discovered, usually too late, that it was only in a version of it that the participants had not yet learned to recognize.

The operation, stated plainly:

Collective uncertainty is converted into performative certainty through the coordinated destruction of a legible target.

Every element of that sentence is doing specific work.

Collective uncertainty is the fuel. The machine runs on the accumulated discomfort of individuals who cannot tolerate their own not-knowing — about the world, about the moral status of their own choices, about the meaning of events that resist categorization, about the coherence of the groups they belong to. Any group of humans generates this discomfort continuously. Most cultures have historically had containers for it — religious ritual, communal narrative, elder authority, tradition — that let the uncertainty be metabolized without producing casualties. When those containers weaken or are absent, the uncertainty accumulates in the group with nowhere to discharge.

Performative certainty is what the operation manufactures. The participants do not become individually more certain. They become collectively performative of certainty in a way that lets each of them pretend, to themselves and to each other, that the private uncertainty has been resolved. The performance is the resolution. Nothing has actually been understood. But the group's shared behavior has produced the felt experience of clarity, which for the participants is functionally indistinguishable from having achieved actual clarity.

Coordinated destruction is the mechanism. The manufacture of performative certainty requires an object on which the group can perform its cohesion. The object has to be visible enough to serve as focus. It has to be portrayable as threat, or as failure, or as offense, or as impurity. It does not have to have actually done anything. It has to be available for the group to converge on. Once the convergence begins, the object is progressively stripped of any complicating detail that would interfere with the group's need for a stable target, and the group's coordination intensifies until the object is destroyed, expelled, silenced, or otherwise processed out of the field.

A legible target is what the object becomes when the operation is running. Legibility is not the same as wrongdoing. A person can be legible because of who they are, what they perceive, what they wear, what they make, what they refuse. Legibility is simply visibility to the group's classification apparatus. Once you are legible, the machine can process you, and the processing feels to the participants like justice, and it produces destruction as its output regardless of whether destruction was warranted.

This is the machine. It has been running for centuries. It is running now.

Part Four

The Reference Case

The clearest documented instance of the operation, and the one the contemporary reader is least likely to defend, is the European and colonial witch trials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The reference is used not because the trials were the first instance — they were not — but because they are the case in which the machine's operation is most extensively studied and least protected by ideological loyalty.

The mechanism in that era was recognizable in every particular. A community accumulating uncertainty — over crop failure, plague, war, theological anxiety, the terror of a changing economic order — would develop an intolerable pressure that had nowhere authorized to go. A person in the community would become legible to the classification apparatus of the moment: a woman living alone, a healer, a midwife, someone whose perception exceeded the container the community had for it, a person whose refusal of ordinary social patterns had made them visible. The accusation would begin. Once the accusation began, the community would converge on the target. Each participant, individually uncertain about whether the accusation was warranted, would find their uncertainty resolved by the collective performance of certainty. The target would be stripped of complicating detail, reduced to the accusation itself, and processed through whatever ritual the era authorized for the destruction. The community would experience itself as clarified, unified, protected. The pressure would be temporarily discharged. Some months or years later, the pressure would accumulate again, and another target would be selected.

Every participant in this operation was, individually, a person who would in ordinary circumstances have been horrified at the idea of participating in the torture and killing of a neighbor. The machine did not require them to be individually evil. It required only that the collective certainty-generating operation be available to convert their private discomfort into shared performance. Most of them died believing they had done what was required. The historical judgment that they had participated in a mass atrocity would not be available to them until the machine had moved on to newer targets and its previous cycle could be examined from outside.

The participants were not evil. They were people without containers being handed an operation that converted their discomfort into felt righteousness. This is the specific feature of the machine that has to be understood before anything else in this essay makes sense. The mob is not a special kind of person. The mob is what ordinary people become when the machine has been made available to them and no counter-container is present.

That is the reference case. What follows is the same operation, running with different infrastructure, on different substrates, under different vocabularies of legitimation, in the present.

Part Five

The Infrastructure Evolution

The machine has never been retired. What has changed, across four centuries, is the infrastructure that carries it. Each infrastructure change has removed a specific source of friction that previously slowed the operation, and each has increased the machine's speed, its scale, and its resistance to correction.

The infrastructure, in stages
Pitchfork Physical mob. Requires assembly, geography, face-to-face confrontation. Slow to organize, limited in reach, moderated by the somatic weight of being present at the destruction.
↓
Pamphlet Printed accusation. Allows coordination beyond the physical village. Removes the requirement of being present. Still moderated by literacy, publication cost, distribution time.
↓
Newspaper Mass daily accusation. Extends the machine's reach to the scale of the modern city. Introduces the professional class of accuser. Still moderated by editorial infrastructure and the delay between event and print.
↓
Broadcast Simultaneous mass accusation. Introduces the felt experience of shared national response. McCarthyism, coordinated media campaigns, televised public shaming. Moderated only by the small number of institutions that controlled the channels.
↓
Platform Real-time distributed accusation at planetary scale. Every remaining friction — physical distance, coordination cost, professional gatekeeping, somatic feedback, temporal delay — has been engineered out. The machine now runs friction-free, at the speed of the algorithm, on any target the classification apparatus makes visible.

Each stage did not replace the previous one. Each was added to it. The current version of the machine has access to every prior technology and runs them in parallel. A target selected by platform algorithm can be reinforced by professional media, amplified by broadcast, and finalized by physical confrontation in the target's home. The complete instrument is more powerful than at any point in the operation's history, and the moderating frictions that used to slow prior versions have almost entirely disappeared.

This matters because it changes what individual participation feels like. Previous versions required the participant to be somewhere, to say something out loud, to be seen doing it, to sign their name. Each of these requirements engaged the participant's somatic sense of moral weight. The current version requires none of them. A participant can contribute to the destruction of a target while never meeting them, never speaking to them, never being seen doing it, and never experiencing any of the physical discomfort that would previously have registered as conscience. The infrastructure has removed the channels through which participation used to feel like anything. Participants often report, with what appears to be sincerity, that they have not done anything harmful. The infrastructure is the reason they can say this. It has quietly stripped the operation of the sensations that would have made them notice they were inside it.

Part Six

Why the Current Site

The machine is running in every culture that has a platform infrastructure, which by now is every culture on earth. What varies is the efficiency with which specific industries and communities produce conditions the machine can exploit. The Kpop industry, at present, is producing those conditions with unusual efficiency, for structural reasons that have nothing to do with the character of Korean fans and everything to do with how the industry itself is organized.

The industrial production of legible sensitives

The Kpop industry produces, as its primary output, extremely visible young people whose entire economic value depends on remaining legible to a mass audience. Trainees are recruited young, often as children, and shaped over years of training into figures whose public presence is highly polished, highly consistent, and highly available for the audience to project onto. The industry structure requires the artist to be constantly perceptible — social media presence, fan communication events, filmed daily life, choreographed public appearances. Privacy is contractually restricted. The artist's body, expressions, statements, and relationships are surfaces that the industry sells and the audience consumes. This is not incidental to the business model. It is the business model.

The result is a class of people whose legibility to the audience is total, whose availability for classification is complete, and whose ability to withdraw from visibility is contractually and financially foreclosed. From the machine's standpoint, this is the ideal target profile. The target is already located, already indexed, already tagged with sufficient personal detail to permit any preferred accusation.

The para-social intensity of the fan relationship

The industry deliberately cultivates a para-social relationship between artists and fans that would have been considered unusually intense in any prior era of pop production. The fan is invited, through the specific vocabulary and rituals of the fandom, to experience the relationship with the artist as personal, reciprocal, and morally weighted. This creates enormous emotional value for the fan under ordinary conditions. It also creates the specific psychological substrate that the machine requires to operate: a mass of people who each individually feel that the artist's actions have personal, moral relevance to their own lives, and who are therefore available to convert any perceived deviation by the artist into felt personal injury.

The platform architecture

Kpop fandom operates almost entirely through the platform infrastructures that have engineered friction out of the machine. Coordinated response to any perceived violation can be organized in minutes, executed in hours, and scaled to millions of participants within a single news cycle. The industry's own infrastructure amplifies this. Companies monitor fan sentiment in real time and adjust artist behavior in response. The machine does not have to reach the artist through indirect channels. It has direct feedback into the systems that determine the artist's continued existence in the industry.

These three structural features — industrial production of legibility, cultivated para-social intensity, platform infrastructure with no residual friction — are what make the Kpop industry, at present, one of the most efficient current sites for the machine's operation. The efficiency is not a feature of the fans. It is a feature of the system the fans are moving through. The system was built by industrial actors for economic reasons. The machine took advantage of what was built. The result is the pattern the fandom has been living inside and grieving from for the past two decades.

Part Seven

The Mob Is Not the Fandom

The most important paragraph in this essay is the one that draws the following distinction, and everything downstream fails if the distinction is not understood.

The vast majority of Kpop fans are not participants in the machine. They love the artists. They engage with the music, the choreography, the community, the shared aesthetic pleasure. They do the tens of thousands of small kindnesses that constitute an actual fandom — the collaborative subtitle projects, the birthday donations to charities, the emotional support networks, the creative work, the years-long friendships formed across languages and continents. The majority of what a fandom is has nothing to do with the operation this essay is naming.

The mob is not the fandom. The mob is an operation that runs through portions of the fandom, using fandom infrastructure and fandom vocabulary, without being fandom itself. At any given time, only a small percentage of a fandom's active members are inside the operation. The rest are doing something else. But the operation is loud enough, coordinated enough, and given enough algorithmic amplification that it can appear from outside — and sometimes from inside — to be the fandom's actual voice.

The people who lose themselves to the mob mindset are not evil. Many of them are people who would, in any other context, be recognized as thoughtful and generous. They are people without containers, being handed an operation that converts their discomfort into felt righteousness inside a community that has become their primary social environment. Naming this is not exonerating them. It is describing accurately what the machine does to good people, so that good people can recognize the operation in themselves and step out of it before they become instruments of it.

Every fandom this essay might describe contains, at any moment, both the fans who are quietly doing the fandom's actual work of love and the participants in the machine's current operation. The essay is not talking about the first. The essay is talking about the second. The distinction is critical and it belongs to the fandom itself to maintain. No one outside the fandom can draw it accurately. The fandom knows which of its own members are inside the operation and which are doing something else. That internal knowledge is one of the counter-technologies the essay will get to.

Part Eight

What the Machine Actually Does

Understanding what the machine is doing, at the level of its actual operation on human minds, is what allows the operation to be recognized and refused. The framework analysis, said as tightly as possible:

The mob is not processing information. It is generating the felt experience of certainty for participants who cannot tolerate their own uncertainty. This is the essential clarification. Every reader who assumes the mob is trying to establish what is true, or to enforce justice, or to correct harm, is misreading the operation. The operation is not epistemic. It is affective. The output is not knowledge. The output is a specific emotional state — the felt experience of shared certainty — that the participants require and that ordinary discourse cannot reliably produce for them.

The target is not the point. This is the second essential clarification. The target is the substrate on which the group performs its cohesion. The target's actual identity, actions, and character are not relevant to what the operation is doing. The target has to be legible, has to be portrayable as violation, has to be available for convergence. Beyond that, the target's specifics do not affect the operation. This is why the same operation can select wildly different targets across different episodes and produce identical dynamics. The operation is indifferent to who is at the center. It requires only that someone be there.

Legibility is not wrongdoing. This is the third essential clarification, and it is the one most readers resist most strongly, because accepting it forces a reevaluation of every previous instance of the operation the reader has been part of. A person's visibility to the classification apparatus is a function of their perceptibility, their availability, their aesthetic legibility, their statistical prominence — not their behavior. Targets are selected by the operation's need for a target, not by an audit of the population for actual wrongdoing. Once the mob has begun to converge on someone, the "evidence" of their wrongness will be located, constructed, or fabricated with whatever effort is required to justify the convergence. The convergence is the cause; the accusations are the effect.

The operation is not doing what it appears to be doing. It is not seeking truth. It is not enforcing justice. It is not correcting harm. It is generating shared certainty inside participants who need certainty and cannot generate it privately. The target absorbs the discomfort the group cannot hold. That is the operation. Everything else is post-hoc legitimation.

Once this is seen, the operation cannot be unseen. Its previous instances become visible in memory. Its current instances become visible in real time. And, critically, the participant's own participation in prior operations becomes visible in a way it was not before, which is the beginning of the counter-technology.

Part Nine

The Same Operation, Elsewhere

Kpop is one current site. It is not the only one. The essay names a few adjacent instances to confirm that the operation is not culture-specific but is running everywhere the infrastructure permits.

In 2024, the mobile game Love and Deepspace released a character named Valko whose design failed to match the projection the game's Chinese fan demographic had developed for what a romance-game male lead should look like. The response was a coordinated mob action against the developer, MiHoYo, that included mass review-bombing, refund campaigns, harassment of company employees, and demands for the character's redesign or removal. The developer, faced with the scale of the coordinated response, effectively capitulated. The operation was successful in the sense that the machine produced its intended output. Valko had done nothing. The developer had done nothing that most companies would consider a violation. The operation ran anyway because the target had become legible and the machine had access to the infrastructure required to converge on it.

In Western contexts, the same operation runs continuously against public figures, small business owners, academics, journalists, and private individuals whose surfaces have become legible to whichever current fraction of the platform ecosystem is currently generating an accusation. The vocabulary varies — sometimes progressive, sometimes reactionary, sometimes moralistic, sometimes ideological — but the operation is identical. Coordinated destruction of a legible target as a means of generating shared certainty for participants who cannot hold their private uncertainty. The Western observer who sees this in Kpop mob operations and does not see it in their own political and cultural life is running the operation without recognizing it. The Kpop fan who sees this in Western cancel operations and does not see it in their own fandom is doing the same.

The machine does not care about the participants' politics, culture, or affiliations. It runs on whatever fuel is available. The specific vocabulary of legitimation is post-hoc. What is being defended, in every case, is not a value. What is being defended is the participant's felt experience of shared certainty, and the mechanism for defending it is always the same: locate a legible target, converge, destroy, disperse, repeat.

Part Ten

The Counter-Technology

Naming the operation is the beginning. Refusing to participate in it is the operation of the counter-technology. But the counter-technology has a specific structure that has to be understood before it can be practiced, because most of what people intuitively reach for as opposition to the machine turns out to be participation in it under a different name.

Opposition does not work. Direct opposition to the machine provides the machine with new targets. Opposing a mob by attacking its participants converts the opposer into a producer of the same operation, now aimed at a different demographic. The mob's frame is the fight, and any fight in that frame reproduces the frame. Whoever wins the fight is running the same machine.

Reasoning does not work. The machine is not epistemic. It is affective. Reasoning with the participants of an active operation does not reach the operation, because the operation is not what the participants think they are doing. They believe they are engaged in a moral response to a real harm. The reasoner appears to them as an obstacle to that response, and is processed accordingly. The reasoner becomes a new legible target, and the machine adds them to its queue.

Silence does not work either. Silence in the face of the operation looks, from outside, like complicity, and can be recruited by the operation as evidence of the target's isolation. Silence also does nothing to build the container the counter-technology requires.

What works — the only thing that has ever worked — is the development of containers that can hold what the machine cannot hold. The perceiver who has learned to receive their own discomfort long enough to actually understand what has arrived, rather than converting the discomfort into an accusation, cannot be conscripted by the machine. The reader who has developed the capacity to sit with a piece of art, a public figure, a person whose behavior violates a current norm, without needing to route the discomfort of not-knowing through the machinery of collective certainty-generation, is one participant the machine will fail to acquire. Every such reader is a small piece of infrastructure competing with the machine's infrastructure for the perceiver's operating system.

This is what the framework this archive has been building actually is. Not a theory. A container. Every essay is a demonstration that discomfort can be held. That reception can precede judgment. That pattern-matching is not the same as understanding. That the person whose surface has become legible to the machine is often not who the machine has classified them as. Each essay is one small piece of counter-infrastructure being made publicly available. Anyone who reads and feels the pattern of their own attention emerging is being handed the tools to run their own reception rather than participating in the collective processing that would otherwise absorb them.

The counter-technology is not opposition to the machine. It is the offer of a different operating system for the participant. The machine runs on participants. Every participant who leaves the machine and enters the counter-technology is one fewer target the machine has, and one more small container in the world capable of holding what the machine cannot.

The fandom is one of the great potential sites for this counter-technology. The reason is precisely the internal knowledge named earlier — that the fandom itself knows which of its own members are inside the operation and which are doing something else. That distinguishing knowledge is not available to outsiders. It is available to fans. The fandom's own capacity to name the mob inside itself, to refuse to be spoken for by the operation running through it, to hold its own members accountable to something other than the machine — is the strongest possible position from which the counter-technology can be practiced. Fans have the standing to say this is not us in a way no outside critic ever will. Every time a fandom does this out loud, in public, in numbers, the machine loses ground it cannot easily reclaim.

Part Eleven

The Naming

Once the machine is named, it cannot hold the same power over people who have learned to recognize it. This is why the essay exists. Not to defeat the machine — the machine is too structurally embedded and too well-adapted to current infrastructure to be defeated by any single essay or any single argument. The essay exists to hand vocabulary to the specific readers whose reception is intact enough to use it.

Every such reader who recognizes the operation is a target the machine will fail to conscript. Every fan who, next time an accusation begins to spread, notices the specific pattern of collective-uncertainty-converting-to-performative-certainty happening in real time, is a participant who quietly opts out. Every friend who sees the pattern in another friend and gently names it — look at what is actually happening here, and what your part in it is — is a piece of counter-infrastructure being installed one relationship at a time.

This is the work. Not stopping the machine. Building the containers, one reader at a time, that let people opt out of it, and giving those readers the vocabulary they need to recognize the operation when it begins to run inside their own communities. The machine has always had participants faster than the counter-technology has had containers. That has not changed. What has changed, occasionally, in specific eras, is that a sufficient density of containers gets built in a specific community that the machine can no longer run at full efficiency there. Those are the eras historians later describe as periods of cultural maturity or moral progress. They are not that. They are periods when enough containers happened to be present at the same time that the machine's fuel — the collective uncertainty of participants without containers — briefly ran short.

The archive this essay belongs to is being built, in part, to help produce one of those periods. Not in every community. Not on every subject. In whatever subset of the reading public happens to receive the framework and find that it names something they have been living without vocabulary for. That subset is not large. It does not have to be. The counter-technology has always been the work of relatively small numbers of people who happened to be present at the moment the operation was running, who recognized what was happening, and who quietly refused to fuel it.

Part Twelve

Coda

The essay opened by refusing to name them. It closes the same way.

The people the machine has taken from the fandom are held in the memory of the people who loved them. That memory does not need this essay to preserve it. The fandom will remember. The industry will not always. The platforms will not. The mob that took them has already moved on to other targets and will not remember the names by which those targets were once known. But the people who loved them will remember, and that memory is a form of counter-infrastructure the machine cannot reach.

What this essay adds is a name for what took them. Not a name for them. A name for the operation. A name that lets the fans who have been living inside the operation without vocabulary for it finally have language equal to what they have been carrying. The fandom already knew. This essay is not telling the fandom anything the fandom did not already know. What the essay is doing is putting into shared language a knowledge that has previously been individual, so that it can become collective, so that the counter-technology can begin to be practiced together rather than alone.

The tradition of receptive perceivers who refused to participate in the machine's operations is four centuries long. It has never been defeated. It has always been building. It has always been the smaller and quieter of the two operations, and it has always been sufficient to preserve, from era to era, the possibility of a way of being with each other that does not require the destruction of legible targets to sustain itself. That tradition is what the archive belongs to. This essay is one more small addition to it.

The machine has been running for four hundred years.
The counter-technology has been running for four hundred years too.
Both are still running now.
You are one of them. Which one is your choice.
